
Source book: Tom Hougaard, *Best Loser Wins: Why Normal Thinking Never Wins the Trading Game* (Harriman House, 2022). A trading psychology book by a professional spread-betting/futures day trader, focused on the behavioral/mental side of trading rather than chart patterns — the direct complement to `reference_al_brooks_trends`, `reference_al_brooks_ranges`, and `reference_al_brooks_reversals` within `project_trade` stage 1 (learning analysis). Read and distilled 2026-09-06.

Author background (relevant to weighing his claims): Danish, BSc Economics/MSc Finance, worked at JPMorgan Chase, blew up a home-trading account in 18 months (2000-01), became a broker/analyst at Financial Spreads (fired after correctly calling Marconi to zero, offending long-holding clients), spent ~7-8 years on the City Index trading floor observing tens of millions of retail trades, made redundant in 2009, has traded for himself since. Self-describes as a high-stake day trader (stakes from £100 to £3,500+ per point; has traded >£250m notional value in a day). Publishes a timestamped, real-time public track record rather than after-the-fact case studies — this is the credibility claim underpinning the whole book.


## Core Thesis

Hougaard's central claim: **trading failure is not a knowledge problem, it is a behavioral/psychological problem.** Roughly 70-90% of retail CFD/spread-betting/FX accounts lose money (he cites broker-disclosed EU failure-rate stats: IG 75%, Markets.com 89%, CMC Markets 75%, Saxo Bank 74%, FX Pro 77%, correct as of Nov 2019). This happens despite spreads, tools, education, and platform quality having improved dramatically over 20+ years — proof that the bottleneck isn't technical infrastructure or technical-analysis knowledge.

His diagnosis: **traders who fail are "normal"** — normal, well-adjusted, intelligent, hard-working people applying normal consumer psychology to a domain where it is actively destructive. "Normal" instincts (seek bargains, avoid what's expensive, hope things improve, protect against pain, cut winners short and let losers run) produce the 90% failure rate almost mechanically. To win, you must think and act in a way that is *not* normal.

**"Best loser wins"** is his literal thesis, not a slogan: in most professions a ~50-60% success/hit rate would be a disaster (a dentist or surgeon with a 60% success rate would be shut down). In trading, a trader can be *wrong on roughly half of all trades* and still be highly profitable — *if* they have trained themselves to lose well: cheaply, unemotionally, without revenge-seeking, without disrupting the next decision. His own best setups only work about 70% of the time; even after decades he is "wrong" roughly 30-50% of the time depending on the strategy. His edge is not superior chart-reading or a secret system — "my knowledge of technical analysis is average at best... my knowledge of myself is what sets me apart." He states outright: "I am exceptionally good at losing... in trading, unlike life, the best loser wins."

A key supporting proof-point he returns to repeatedly: a study of **43 million trades executed by 25,000 retail FX clients over 15 months** (analyst David Rodriguez) found clients won 61-62% of their trades (a genuinely good hit rate) but averaged ~43 pips per win versus ~83 pips per loss. Net effect on 100 trades: 61 winners × 43 = 2,623 pips gained; 39 losers × 83 = 3,237 pips lost — a net loss *despite* a winning hit rate. His conclusion: these traders are good at analysis (picking direction) and bad at psychology (managing the position once it's open, especially cutting losers and letting winners run). Hit rate is close to irrelevant; **it's what you do with wins and losses, size-wise, that determines the outcome.**

Counter-proof used to reinforce the same point from the other direction: a hedge-fund friend's strategy (traded on the DeMark Sequential indicator, 1-minute South African equities) had only a 20-30% hit rate but was "wildly successful" because winners averaged ~25x the size of losers, and the strategy had a hard rule that a trade must start working *immediately* or be closed. Conclusion: **expectancy, not accuracy, is what matters** — and there are many legitimate ways to construct positive expectancy.


## Risk & Loss-Taking Framework

- **There is no "cheap" or "expensive" in markets** — only the prevailing price. Treating a falling asset like a supermarket discount ("buy toilet paper at 50% off") is his single most-repeated psychological trap. "The financial markets are not a supermarket." Real anecdotes used to hammer this: his own 2001 on-air call that Marconi (already down from 1,200p to 450p) was "garbage" and would go to zero (it did — he was fired for saying so, then vindicated); Northern Rock in 2007 (a stranger phoned him on a Saturday morning asking if buying the collapsing "too big to fail" bank was smart — Hougaard warned him off; Northern Rock went bust, the first UK bank run in a century); Jim Cramer declaring Bear Stearns "fine" on air days before its collapse in 2008.
- **Paul Tudor Jones anecdote** (used as the model of correct behavior): when the market suddenly gapped down hard, PTJ instantly dumped his longs and flipped to short into the fall, indifferent to the fact that price had "already fallen so far." Quote: *"Forget cheap. Forget expensive. It's just numbers on a page... If it kept going down, I'd sell it to zero. If it kept going up, I'd buy it to infinity."* The lesson is mental agility — the ability to flip from long-conviction to short-conviction instantly, without ego, when the market disagrees with you.
- **Trend-following bias, deliberately unapologetic**: Buy strength, sell weakness — "buy high, sell higher; sell low, buy back lower" (an explicit inversion of "buy low, sell high"). "Top pickers and bottom pickers soon become cotton pickers." He accepts he will never catch exact tops/bottoms and considers this an acceptable, even necessary, cost.
- **The "trading mind upside-down" problem**: normal traders feel FEAR when winning (fear the profit will vanish) and HOPE when losing (hope it reverses) — backwards. Correct psychology: be *hopeful* when winning (there may be much more to come) and *fearful* when losing (cut it, because the market is telling you you're wrong).
- **Cognitive dissonance (Festinger) framework**: the mind resolves the discomfort of a *profitable* position by closing it (removing the "threat" of losing the gain), and resolves the discomfort of a *losing* position by holding it (avoiding the finality of crystallizing pain). Both reactions are backward from what's rational and are driven by pain-avoidance, not market logic.
- **"Everybody gets what they want from the markets" (Ed Seykota, quoted)**: a losing trader is not motivated to actually change — genuine change only follows real behavioral effort, and most people's stated desire to trade well is not matched by their actual effort (illustrated by an anecdote of a friend who asked for help reviewing his trading statements, then went silent for days/years).
- **Hope vs. fear asymmetry**: Hope is felt only while *in* a losing trade (hoping it turns around) — "hope dies last," which is why losers are held far longer than logic dictates. Fear is felt both in and out of a position (fear of missing a move, or regret at exiting too soon). He counters hope with a hard pre-set stop-loss decided *before* the trade, and counters both fear types with extensive morning visualization/rehearsal (see Rules & Routines below).
- **Loss aversion, explicitly cited**: "we experience a financial loss 250% more intensely than an equivalent financial gain" — used to justify a personal practice of trying to flatten his emotional response to both wins and losses equally, rather than swing between euphoria and despair ("I win. Move on. I lose. Move on.").
- **Deliberately embraces (rather than avoids) trading pain**: unlike ordinary emotional pain where "a problem shared is a problem halved," he says with trading pain he does the opposite — "I run towards it. I embrace it... I need it" — treating discomfort as the signal that he's doing the correct, unnatural thing.


## Adding to Winners / Letting Profits Run (his signature technique)

This is presented as the single most important behavioral inversion in the book, sourced from two mentor figures:

- **Charlie DiFrancesca ("Charlie D"), legendary CBOT bond-pit trader, Hougaard's "greatest trading hero"**: *"The time you know you've become a good trader is that first day you were able to win by holding and adding to a winning position."* His mentor Everett Klipp: it's human nature to cut winners short and let losers ride — the opposite of correct trading.
- **Dr. David Paul (personal mentor)**, at a 2007 Johannesburg workshop, gave Hougaard the reframe that changed his trading: *"When you are in a winning position, instead of thinking where to get out, why don't you think about where to get in more?"* This directly inverts the standard retail approach of scaling out / taking half profits.

**Why it's psychologically hard to add to winners but easy to add to losers** (his explicit breakdown): adding to a loser feels "logical" — you get a better average price, and you can dwell on the potential for a bigger eventual profit while ignoring that the market currently disagrees with you. Adding to a winner feels risky — you dwell on the fact your average price has worsened (more give-back potential) while ignoring that the market currently agrees with you. He argues this is backward: you should dwell on what the market is telling you (right/wrong), not on your average entry price.

**Practical rules for pyramiding into winners:**
- Always place a stop-loss on the *added* position too — same principle as the first entry.
- Two pyramiding styles: (1) same-size adds (his own preference — riskier), or (2) decreasing-size adds (recommended for those newer to the technique — first position biggest, each subsequent add smaller).
- Move the stop-loss on the *original* position up (in profit direction) each time you add, so the combined position's total risk stays bounded rather than compounding unchecked.
- Worked numeric framework: define **N** = Average True Range appropriate to the instrument/session/timeframe you actually trade (he stresses ATR varies hugely by time of day — e.g., FTSE 5-min ATR ~4 points overnight vs. ~14 points at the 8am open). Stop-loss = 2N. Risk per trade = a fixed % of account (example: 2% of £10,000 = £200). Position-size unit = monetary risk ÷ stop distance in points. Add to the position at every ½N move in your favor, tightening/raising the stop correspondingly on each add.
- He also uses a "flip stop": when pyramided deep into a winning short, for example, he'll place the stop at the price level where he'd actually want to become a *buyer* — i.e., the stop doubles as his reversal trigger.
- Acknowledges the real downside with a documented losing example: pyramiding a DAX short that reversed against him, turning a big open profit into a real loss — used deliberately to show pyramiding is not free money and requires active stop management.
- Never sets profit targets. Explicitly rejects the standard "risk:reward ratio" framework taught in most trading education: *"I believe in defining my risk. I don't believe in defining my reward... losers spend their time thinking how much they will make, while winners spend their time thinking about how much they will lose."* The only variable a discretionary (non-algorithmic) trader fully controls is how much to risk — reward is whatever the market gives.
- Explicitly rejects "taking half profits" (a very common retail practice) on two grounds: (1) if the market agrees with you, ride it; (2) since reward can't be known in advance, artificially capping it via partial exits forecloses the rare "home-run" trades that "sustain you in this business" and produce the big months.

**Note for `project_trade`:** this directly conflicts with Al Brooks' recommended scale-out ladder (exit ~50% at 2R, ~25% at 3R, trail the rest — see `reference_al_brooks_ranges` Trade Management). Worth resolving deliberately (e.g., Brooks' approach may fit range/scalp-heavy trading better, Hougaard's fits strong-trend/swing trading better) rather than silently picking one.


## Position Sizing & Money Management

- **Only the loss side is controllable.** Define maximum risk per trade as a fixed % of account equity (his worked example uses 2%), derived from the instrument's own recent volatility (ATR/N), not an arbitrary point count.
- **Two clear rules stated as opposites of what "the 90%" do**: (1) never add to a losing position — under any circumstances; (2) always add to a winning position (at minimum, in small increments) once your first position is in profit and the market is confirming you.
- **Never trade without a stop-loss** — cited (via a 30-year CFD-industry CEO he interviews) as one of the five clearest predictors of a client's eventual failure, alongside: opening an account under £100, overtrading/high frequency, the ratio of time spent holding winners vs. losers, and whether the trader adds to winners or to losers.
- **Trade size should grow slowly, in step with the trader's psychological capacity**, not in one jump — analogized to training for a marathon or to Philippe Petit's seven years of preparation before his high-wire walks. Trading big size before you're mentally conditioned for the resulting pain/pleasure swings is a common failure mode.
- **Negative balance protection** (now a legal requirement in much of Europe) is noted as a real historical risk that used to allow accounts (and traders) to go far more deeply negative than today.


## Probability, Uncertainty, and the Single Trade vs. the Sample

This is one of the most load-bearing ideas in the book:

- **"There is randomness in the outcome of one, but there is order in the outcome of 100"** (quoting David Paul). A single trade is essentially a coin flip — you cannot know in advance whether it wins or loses, no matter how good the setup looks. But behavior applied *consistently* across 100 trades produces a statistically predictable, and potentially highly profitable, result.
- Illustrated by his own experiment: he flipped a coin 100 times and got a run of 15 heads in a row — long enough that he checked the coin for a physical defect, even though he intellectually knew the odds of heads on flip #16 were still 50/50. He uses this to argue that a run of 15 losing (or winning) trades feels meaningful and threatens to distort judgment, but *is not evidence of anything about the next trade.*
- **Practical implication**: your behavior (whether you follow your stop, whether you add to winners, whether you add to losers) must be identical on every single trade regardless of the last trade's outcome, because the edge only manifests over a large sample. Trading "against" a losing streak (revenge trading, doubling up) or trading scared after several losses both violate this.
- **Corollary rule he applies to himself**: he does not stop trading after a string of losses (he references having had four losing days in a row, and reviewed his May 2020 month where he lost on 66 of 137 trades — under 50% — yet still netted +1,513 points). His explicit position: stopping trading after three losing trades in a row "is a flawed approach if you understand the markets" — the only valid reasons to stop are being physically ill or emotionally compromised, not a short losing run.
- **"Assume you are wrong until proven otherwise"** — presented as one of his four core operating rules (see Rules and Routines). This is the practical, trade-by-trade expression of taking uncertainty seriously: entering with the working assumption of being wrong (rather than the 90%'s implicit assumption of being right) changes what you do when the market disagrees with you — you exit fast rather than defend the position.


## Rules, Routines, and Concrete Practices

### The four core rules ("Flip the Switch")
His most distilled operating checklist, stated explicitly as the practical crux of the book:
1. **I assume I am wrong — until proven otherwise.**
2. **I expect to be uncomfortable.**
3. **I add when I am right.**
4. **I never add when I am wrong.**

### The "Book of Truths" (his central, named practice)
A concrete, repeatable self-audit process:
1. Export your **entire trading history** into a spreadsheet.
2. Categorize trades exhaustively — by time of day, day of week, hold duration, setup type, market condition (trending vs. range-bound), etc. — until real behavioral patterns emerge (as opposed to assumed ones).
3. Write down your findings honestly, however unflattering. His own documented findings included: periods with an 85%+ win rate that still weren't very profitable; average winners smaller than average losers; strong morning performance but afternoon give-back; strong Monday-Thursday performance with Friday give-back; good performance in range-bound markets but consistently missing (or actively fighting) trend days — and his single biggest source of losses was fighting trending moves.
4. Build a **visual "warm-up" file** (he uses PowerPoint) plotting his own actual past trades — both the disastrous impulsive ones and the well-executed ones — on their charts, and review it every single morning before trading, deliberately re-living both the shame of bad trades and the satisfaction of good ones. He argues this visual, emotionally vivid self-confrontation is far more behavior-changing than written reminders/mantras ("Don't trade without a stop-loss" on a Post-it does little by comparison).

### Morning mental-preparation routine (concrete, repeated across the book)
- Sits quietly (earplugs if needed), and **vividly visualizes losing a specific, personally meaningful sum of money** (e.g., the cost of a car or his son's tuition, or a real past loss like £78,000) — lets the emotional pain fully register — then flips to visualizing *winning* the same amount, noting deliberately that the imagined win never produces equal-and-opposite joy (used as direct, felt evidence of loss aversion). Also rehearses: entering a trade and calmly cutting the loss; watching a big profit shrink and accepting it calmly; a sudden adverse news shock and reversing without panic.
- Uses a breathing exercise (in for 7 seconds, out for 11, for 5-15 minutes) to calm the mind before or during volatile sessions, plus deliberately stressful mental imagery (dangerous animals, cliffs, big surf) to practice staying calm under simulated high arousal — a technique he links to Formula 1 drivers' pre-race routines.
- Prints and reviews charts of his core instruments on **two timeframes** (e.g., 5-minute and 10-minute) each evening — the slower chart is used specifically to counteract the overtrading impulse the faster chart produces.

### "Ask for Help" (belief-diffusion journaling exercise)
Sit with a blank page, pose a specific question about a recurring fear or bad habit (e.g., "Why am I afraid to join a downtrend after it's already started?"), free-write without censorship for 10-20 minutes, and do not judge what surfaces — the goal is to defuse the belief's emotional charge (fighting a belief entrenches it further; accepting it lets it go) and to replace it with a short, concrete, one-sentence rule (e.g., "I will wait for the first ten-minute bar to close before deciding to trade").

### "Twenty Trades" exercise (from Dr. David Paul)
Take the next 20 trade signals exactly as they appear, with no hesitation or override — the goal is not profit (expect roughly breakeven) but to "smoke out" internal conflict. Success is completing all 20 without emotional resistance, having internalized: outcomes are random per-trade; each moment is independent of the last; wins/losses are coin-flip-distributed; you don't need to predict the next move, only control your risk. Repeat until achieved.

### Other stated rules of thumb
- Never trades with a profit target on the initial plan — "I want to see what the market will give me," even if it means giving back some open profit (he cites regularly giving back 100-point Dow winners to zero as an accepted cost of occasionally catching 400-500 point days).
- Reviews every trading day's chart after the close, plotting entries/exits — he found ~8 out of 10 of his own early trades were unplanned "impulse" trades, and reducing impulse trades directly increased profitability.
- Does not stop trading after a short losing streak (see Probability section); does stop if physically unwell or emotionally compromised.
- Recommends anyone considering full-time trading first try it during a week or two of holiday/leave before quitting their job, to get a realistic preview of the lifestyle (isolation, screen time) before committing.
- Explicitly process-oriented, not goal-oriented: no P&L targets, no Post-it notes with money goals — "if I follow the process, the outcome takes care of itself."


## Psychological Traps He Identifies (and how he counters them)

1. **The "supermarket mentality" / value illusion** — treating falling prices as "cheap" (bargain-hunting instinct) and rising prices as "expensive" (avoid buying), when in markets "there is no cheap, there is no expensive, just the prevailing price." Counter: trade with strength/weakness, not against it.
2. **Trying to catch the exact low or exact high** — the single most damaging retail habit he cites from direct floor observation (example: clients repeatedly tried to buy "the low" all through the Lehman Brothers crash day, 15 Sept 2008; a low never came that day or the next). Backed by an IG Markets sentiment report (26 Oct 2021) showing 60-71% of retail positions were short across major indices *on the day those indices made fresh all-time highs*.
3. **Believing every small counter-move against the trend is a new trend starting.** Same root cause as #2.
4. **Apophenia / "patternicity"** — seeing patterns and meaning (trend lines, Fibonacci levels, candlestick formations) that are not statistically real, driven by the brain's pain-avoidance selectively erasing failed setups from memory ("the brain sends a signal to the eyes to ignore the setups that do not work"). Backed by: Larry Williams' decade-long S&P 500 retracement study showing Fibonacci ratios (61.8%, 38.2%) are not disproportionately represented; his own 2016 Oslo research on DAX 78.6% retracements (only 20% hit rate, yet still tradeable with the right risk/reward setup); a 2021 S&P 500 rally where the most frequent retracement percentages were essentially random (43%, 74%) rather than classic Fibonacci numbers; and two cited academic papers finding little/no statistically significant predictive power in candlestick reversal patterns (Jamaloodeen et al. 2018; Tharavanij et al.). Anecdote: a famous candlestick-pattern book author admitted to Hougaard privately that he didn't actually trade at all.
5. **Cutting winners short out of fear, letting losers run on hope** — the central behavioral inversion of the whole book (see Risk framework above).
6. **Overtrading** — out of boredom, or out of anger/desire for revenge.
7. **Revenge trading / "getting even"** — explicitly named as one of his own historical bad patterns; countered by treating each trade as statistically independent (coin-flip framework) and by pre-accepting losses during morning visualization so there's no unresolved emotional charge to avenge.
8. **Averaging down / adding to losing positions** — "we all love a bargain at the supermarket... do not do it in the financial markets."
9. **Moving/removing stop-losses** — treated as a symptom of the subconscious mind hijacking a plan set by the conscious mind; countered by pre-committing to the stop during the morning visualization exercise, before real money and real emotion are engaged.
10. **Reducing size after wins, increasing size after losses ("doubling up")** — cited via Greg Riba (trader, in the documentary *Floored*): "99% still don't get it — when they're winning, they start betting less. Bet more! ... Let the winners ride." The correct behavior is the reverse of instinct in both directions.
11. **Trading yesterday's (or an earlier) emotional state instead of the present chart** — illustrated by a live dialogue with a follower who wanted to close a good DAX trade early specifically to "make up for" a loss from the previous session. Hougaard's diagnosis: "You are trading yesterday's experience... you are not judging the trade on its own merit." Contrasted with the golfer Luke Donald's observation about Tiger Woods: Tiger's real edge wasn't better mechanics but his ability to completely forget the previous bad shot and be fully present for the next one.
12. **Letting an "expert" opinion (yours or someone else's) override what the market is actually doing** — the central lesson of the Dr. David Paul parable: an engineer-trader kept adding to a losing long because his proprietary software and Elliott Wave analysis said the market "had to" rise, ignoring that it was falling in front of him; his wife's naive, repeated question ("but it's going down right now, isn't it?") triggered the realization that "I wasn't trading the market. I was trading my opinion." His new mottoes afterward: "sell low and cover lower," "buy high and sell higher," and the "surrender" idea — trade like a surfer paddling into a wave's actual energy, not like someone trying to impose their will on the ocean.
13. **Confirmation bias / seeing what you've decided to see** — "we don't see things as they are, we see them as we are" (Anaïs Nin, quoted); mentor Bryce Gilmore: "you only see in the markets what you have trained your eyes to see." Illustrated by the story of a friend, "Nick," who turned permanently bearish after reading Zero Hedge in 2004 and kept shorting a rising market on ideological conviction until he stopped trading entirely.
14. **Overconfidence / letting a calm period create complacency** — his own 14-day holiday anecdote: traded very selectively and profitably while relaxed, then gave all of it back within 14 days of returning to the normal floor environment — used to show the "ideal mindset" is fragile and must be actively maintained, not assumed once achieved.
15. **Believing effort and desire are the same thing** — several anecdotes (a friend who asked for help reviewing his statements and then went silent for days; a friend who talked about "increasing trading size" for seven years without ever doing it) used to argue that unfulfilled stated goals usually reveal that the person doesn't actually want the change badly enough, echoing Ed Seykota's line that "a losing trader is not going to want to transform himself — that's the kind of thing winning traders do."


## Notable Anecdotes and Case Studies (with their specific lessons)

- **Black Wednesday, 16 Sept 1992** — Hougaard, age 22, happened to exchange Danish kroner for GBP right as the pound crashed out of the ERM (Soros's Quantum Fund famously made ~$1bn), netting him an extra £4,000 that funded his university education debt-free. Paired immediately with **"the Englishman"** — a London printing-company owner who spotted the same overvalued-franc opportunity via a Paris hotdog price, turned a £5,000 account into £8 million shorting the franc — and then lost it all back and more. Lesson: making the money is not the hard part; *keeping* it (i.e., handling the psychology of winning) is.
- **Marconi (2001)** — Hougaard, then a young analyst, told wealthy clients on a coach to Royal Ascot and later on CNBC that Marconi (already down from 1,200p) was "garbage" heading to zero. It did go to zero; he was fired for the comments but ultimately vindicated. Reused later as a direct parallel to Northern Rock (2007).
- **Northern Rock (2007)** — a stranger phoned Hougaard at home on a Saturday asking whether buying the collapsing bank was wise; Hougaard warned him off on the grounds that (a) there was likely undisclosed bad news, (b) there was no meaningful place for a stop-loss, and (c) buying "because it's cheap" trains the mind to keep catching falling knives. The bank went bust days later, triggering the UK's first bank run in a century.
- **The 43-million-trade / 25,000-client FX study** (analyst David Rodriguez) — the book's core statistical evidence for "hit rate doesn't matter, loss/win size does" (see Core Thesis).
- **Trevor Neil's hedge fund** — traded the Tom DeMark Sequential indicator on 1-minute South African equity charts with only a 20-30% hit rate, but with winners ~25x the size of losers and a strict "must work immediately or exit" rule; "wildly successful" despite the low hit rate.
- **Paul Tudor Jones** — instantly reversing from long to short into a sudden drop, indifferent to "cheap vs. expensive" (see Risk framework above). Model of decisive, ego-free reversal.
- **Dr. David Paul's "software" parable** — an engineer-trader stubbornly held and added to a losing long because his models "proved" the market had to rise; his wife's naive question broke the spell ("I wasn't trading the market, I was trading my opinion"). Source of the "surrender"/surfer metaphor and Hougaard's own adding-to-winners epiphany at Paul's 2007 Johannesburg workshop.
- **Charlie DiFrancesca ("Charlie D")** — CBOT bond-pit legend, Hougaard's "greatest trading hero," source of the "good trading means combatting the emotions that make us human" quote and the core lesson about adding to winners.
- **Adam** — a friend who built a successful 30-minute-chart system (inspired by George Taylor's *The Taylor Trading Technique*) and became wealthy trading full time, but held a maximum short position over a weekend when US forces captured Saddam Hussein; the market opened "limit up" Sunday night, he couldn't cover, his broker force-liquidated the position at a large loss (~£750k account cut to ~£400k) right before the market gave back the gains. Unable to emotionally process the "unfair" loss, he spiraled into revenge trading, lost his house, lied to those around him, and ended up homeless and suicidal; still owes Hougaard £20,000. Used as the book's starkest cautionary tale about weekend/overnight tail risk plus the compounding danger of an unprocessed loss triggering a behavioral collapse.
- **A renowned London surgeon** who lost £750,000 trading forex and broke down crying in Hougaard's brokerage's reception, begging for a loan to trade his way back — used to argue that education/professional status is irrelevant to trading psychology.
- **A 15-year full-time-trader friend** who made a City-managerial-equivalent income with better family time, but eventually felt isolated once his kids grew older and took a broker job instead — used to make the point that "success" in trading can legitimately mean a stable good living, not necessarily riches, and that stepping back from trading is not failure.
- **Hougaard's own 2020 slump** (dated, specific): up ~£200,000 in a month, then lost £33k, £9k, and £38k in three successive sessions (>50% of the month's gains gone) with no identifiable external cause — used to normalize slumps even for elite/experienced traders, and to argue the correct response is to "slow down," not force it, and to trust the process rather than chase the outcome.
- **Kobe Bryant** — as a rookie in 1997 he made four costly late-game errors, then practiced alone all night; he went on to hold the NBA record for *most missed shots in history*. Paired with **Babe Ruth**, who held the home-run record while also being nicknamed "King of Strikeouts." Lesson: elite performance requires a high tolerance for visible, repeated failure, not its avoidance.
- **Philippe Petit** (WTC high-wire artist) — seven years of physical/mental preparation before his walk; overcomes fear via vivid visualization of the best outcome, and via deliberately *exaggerating* imagined fears in advance so the real experience feels comparatively mild; studies his specific fears (e.g., spiders) directly to strip them of power.
- **Elon Musk** — cited for two fear-management tools: (1) an overriding sense of mission/purpose (SpaceX, "we'd be stuck on Earth forever" otherwise) and (2) "fatalism" — consciously accepting the probability of failure in advance (Musk estimated <10% odds of SpaceX succeeding and "just accepted" the likely loss), which paradoxically reduces the fear.
- **Free-diving practice with his son** — improvement came entirely from focusing on process (breath preparation, staying calm, efficient strokes) rather than the outcome/distance goal — direct analogy for why chasing a daily P&L target is self-sabotaging versus focusing on correct execution.
