
Source book: Book 3 of Al Brooks' price-action trilogy (Book 1 = Trends, Book 2 = Trading Ranges). Focuses entirely on how trends end, how to tell a real reversal from a pullback, and why reversal trades are Brooks' least-favored, lowest-probability category of trade despite offering his best risk:reward. Read and distilled 2026-09-06 as part of `project_trade` stage 1 (learning analysis). Companion files: `reference_al_brooks_trends` (book 1), `reference_al_brooks_ranges` (book 2).


## Core Philosophy

Brooks' central, repeated thesis: **"There is really no such thing as a trend reversal pattern."** In a trend, every pattern is a continuation pattern; occasionally one fails, and only in hindsight do traders call that failure a "reversal pattern." Because a trend has *inertia*, **about 80% of all attempts to reverse a trend fail** and become just another flag before the trend resumes (this ~80% figure recurs constantly — for reversal patterns in trends, for breakouts out of the top/bottom of trading ranges, and for topping/bottoming chart patterns like head-and-shoulders on any timeframe). Symmetrically, in a trading range, ~80% of breakout attempts fail and price is pulled back into the range.

Consequently, Brooks' own stated preference is *against* reversal-hunting: "I prefer high-percentage trades... pullback entries and trading range fades... I look less often for reversal trades, because most reversal attempts fail, but I will take a strong reversal setup." Reversal trades are attractive only because when they work, **reward is often several multiples of risk**, which can offset a sub-50% win rate (the "trader's equation": probability × reward must exceed probability of failure × risk).

Two mantras recur dozens of times and function as the book's load-bearing rules:
- **"When the market tries to do something twice and fails both times, it usually then tries to go in the opposite direction."**
- **"Buy low and sell high, except in a clear and strong trend"** (where you instead buy high and exit higher, or sell low and exit lower).


## Terminology — Brooks' Precise Definitions

- **Reversal**: "A change to an opposite type of behavior." Not just bull-to-bear or bear-to-bull — since trading-range behavior is the opposite of trending behavior, a **trend becoming a trading range is also a reversal**. (A trading range becoming a trend is technically a reversal too, but is conventionally called a "breakout.")
- **Trend reversal / major trend reversal**: a change from a bull to bear trend or vice versa, where "the setup must include a test of the old trend extreme after a break of the trend line." The word "major" doesn't imply drama — it's a *relative* term meaning simply that a trend is trying to reverse; on a smaller/larger timeframe the same event might not be called "major."
- **Always in**: if forced to hold a position at all times, whichever side you'd choose right now. Distinguishing an "always-in reversal" (a real change in market control, usually needs a spike/two consecutive strong trend bars to confirm) from a **countertrend scalp** (a low-probability bet within an unchanged trend) is, per Brooks, the single most important skill in trading.
- **Climax**: broadly, *any* unsustainably large/fast move — even one giant trend bar counts. A **climactic reversal** is a climax quickly followed by a sharp move in the opposite direction (functionally a two-bar reversal, sometimes visible only on a higher-timeframe chart).
- **Wedge**: traditionally a three-push move (each push extending a bit further) with the trend line and trend channel line at least minimally convergent. Practically, *any* three-push pattern "trades like a wedge" regardless of exact shape — stairs (parallel lines), wedge (convergent), or expanding triangle (divergent) all behave the same way.
- **Trend channel line (TCL) overshoot / undershoot**: TCL overshoot = price penetrates the far channel boundary (a sign of parabolic acceleration, often the setup for a climactic reversal); undershoot = price approaches but fails to reach the TCL before reversing back into the channel.
- **Final flag**: the last pullback/flag before a trend genuinely ends. Because "every reversal follows some kind of flag," Brooks treats every reversal as, at bottom, a species of final-flag reversal.
- **Failed final flag**: a final-flag reversal signal that triggers but fails within a bar or two — this becomes a **breakout pullback** (a continuation setup) rather than a reversal.
- **Double top / double bottom pullback**: a *reversal* pattern at the very start of a new trend (a double bottom, then a deep higher-low pullback that tests just above it) — as opposed to a **double bottom bull flag** (the visually identical pattern occurring mid-trend as a continuation setup).
- **Expanding triangle**: 5 (sometimes 7, rarely 9) swings, each more extreme than the last, that repeatedly traps breakout traders on both sides until everyone finally capitulates in one direction.
- **High 1/2/3/4, Low 1/2/3/4**: Brooks' bar-counting system for legs within a pullback. High 3 and Low 3 are wedge-variant flags; High 4/Low 4 failing often signals the pullback has become a genuine reversal leg instead.
- **Failure / failed failure**: any trade that doesn't reach its goal. A **failed failure** — a failure that itself fails and resumes the original breakout — is a *second signal*, hence more reliable, and becomes a breakout pullback.


## What Actually Constitutes (and Confirms) a Reversal

### The Four Requirements for a Major Trend Reversal (explicit, numbered in the text)
1. A trend must exist on the chart.
2. A countertrend move strong enough to break the trend line (and usually the moving average).
3. A **test of the trend's extreme**, followed by a **second reversal** — a higher high/double top/lower high at a bull top, or a lower low/double bottom/higher low at a bear bottom.
4. That second reversal must go far enough for a **consensus** that the trend has actually reversed.

The trend-line break alone does NOT constitute a reversal — it only means you should stop fighting the countertrend move and start watching for a test of the old extreme. You should still take with-trend trades first, because the market usually goes back to test the old extreme before anything else happens. Only *after* that test fails a second time should you look for a genuine countertrend swing.

### Real Reversal vs. Pullback — the distinguishing tests
- A pullback is "a temporary pause or countertrend move... that does not retrace beyond the start of the trend, swing, or leg." Once a pullback retraces **75% or more** of the preceding move, Brooks says it's better to think of it as a *new trend in the opposite direction* rather than a pullback in the old one.
- **Momentum of the test leg** is decisive: if the rally testing a bear low is in a tight, steep channel with little overlap and blows past the old extreme, the old trend probably resumes despite the earlier reversal attempt. If the test leg is slow, overlapping, wedge-shaped, and full of counter-colored bodies, odds favor a lower high/higher low and either a range or genuine reversal.
- **Two failed pushes = try the opposite.** A double top or bottom that fails a second breakout attempt is the generic reversal trigger throughout the book — every tradable reversal, even a tiny scalp, "begins with a micro double top or bottom" (traders won't act without one).
- Every reversal pattern is, underneath, some kind of trading range, and trading ranges resolve **with the trend** roughly 80% of the time — so double tops/bottoms, head-and-shoulders, and triangles occurring within a trend are, by base rate, continuation setups (bull/bear flags) that only occasionally become real reversals.


## Reversal Pattern Catalogue

**Climactic reversals** — a spike immediately (or almost immediately) followed by a spike in the opposite direction. Driven by a "vacuum effect": strong countertrend institutions deliberately wait to act until price reaches a level they consider clear value (a magnet — moving average, trend line, measured-move target, round number), and their *absence* lets price race to that level; once there, they act aggressively together with with-trend profit-takers, producing the sharp reversal. Two or three consecutive climaxes without an intervening correction sharply raise the odds of at least a 10-bar, two-legged reversal; the third climax is usually the most dramatic and often marks the actual end. True V-tops/V-bottoms with *no* subsequent test of the spike are rare (a few times a month on a 5-minute chart) — most spike extremes get tested before the market moves on.

**Wedges and three-push patterns** — Brooks explicitly unifies wedges, micro-wedges, parabolic wedges, 4-push wedges, stairs, expanding triangles, triple tops/bottoms, double-top/bottom pullbacks, head-and-shoulders, and failed breakouts of a double top/bottom as *all the same underlying behavior*: trend traders give up after a third failed push, and reversal traders take control. Wedge **pullbacks** (with-trend) can be entered on the first signal; wedge **reversals** (countertrend) should generally wait for a second entry (e.g., buy the higher low after a wedge-bottom's first breakout, not the breakout itself) unless the setup is unusually strong. Targets: first target = a return to the start of the pattern; second target = a measured move using the pattern's height. If the wedge fails (price makes a new extreme beyond it), the market usually runs an approximate measured move in the *opposite* (continuation) direction instead.

**Expanding triangles** — a reversal-or-continuation pattern of progressively more extreme swings. A bottoming expanding triangle traps longs in on a rally above the last swing high, then traps them out (and shorts in) on a new lower low, before reversing hard and forcing both sides to chase. First target: a breakout beyond the *opposite* side of the triangle; if that breakout fails, the pattern flips role (a reversal-type triangle becomes a continuation-type "expanding triangle flag," and vice versa).

**Final flags** — the last flag before a trend ends, generally horizontal with strong two-sided signs (counter-trend bodies, prominent tails, several internal reversals, heavy bar overlap). Can be as small as 1–2 bars, or need no breakout at all in the original trend direction (a bull flag can be the "final flag" of a bull trend even if it never breaks out upward before falling out the bottom). Its breakout is, mechanically, just a second test of the trend's extreme (double-top/bottom logic).

**Double top / double bottom pullbacks** — a *reversal*-role setup: after a double bottom, a higher-low pullback tests just above the double-bottom level, and that pullback is the buy signal. Same visual shape as a double-bottom *bull flag*, but occurring at the *start* of a new trend rather than mid-trend. One of Brooks' most reliable setups when the shape is decent and the final signal bar is strong.

**Head and shoulders** — reframed as fundamentally a *continuation* pattern (a trading range) in Brooks' system: an H&S top in a bull market is usually a wedge bull flag/triangle/triple bottom that fails its downside breakout and resolves upward (measured move based on the right shoulder or whole pattern height); an H&S bottom in a bear market usually resolves downward the same way. Nesting is common (the right shoulder can itself be a smaller H&S or double top).

**Failures** — any trade that doesn't reach its goal (even hitting a smaller-than-planned profit counts). Concrete named failure types: failed High1/2 or Low1/2, failed breakout of anything, failed final flag, failed wedge, one-tick failed breakouts, and specific tick/cent failures (5-tick, 9-tick, 17-tick failures in the Emini; 11¢/51¢/99¢/101¢ failures in stocks). "When a setup fails, it often creates an excellent setup for a trade in the opposite direction" because just-stopped-out traders won't re-enter immediately, leaving the market one-sided. A **failed failure** (a failure that itself fails, resuming the original direction) is a second signal and therefore more reliable — it becomes a breakout pullback.


## Setups for Trading Reversals

**Entry sequence (generic template for any reversal, from opens to intraday to daily charts):** (1) identify a breakout, (2) assess whether it fails into a reversal attempt, (3) check whether *that* failure itself fails (a "failed failure") — which resumes the original direction as a breakout pullback.

**The "best trade" reversal setups explicitly listed by Brooks:**
- **Major trend reversal**: strong trend-line break with a strong reversal bar on the first leg, then buy/sell a *successful test* of the trend's extreme (higher low/double bottom/lower low at a bottom; lower high/double top/higher high at a top) with a strong signal bar on the test itself. "If the setup does not have every one of these components, don't take the trade."
- Strong final-flag reversal after a swing in a trading range/weak channel.
- Buying/selling the 3rd or 4th push in a bear/bull **stairs** pattern for a test of the prior push's extreme.
- Trading when a spike-and-channel's channel (or a trending-trading-range's breakout) reaches a measured-move target and is visibly weakening.
- Shorting the top / buying the bottom of a trading range, **especially on a second entry**.
- (Advanced/limit-order versions) fading a weak follow-through bar right before a would-be always-in flip — e.g., a bear trend "needs one more bull bar" to confirm reversal but the bar looks weak: sell its close, betting the flip fails and the bear trend resumes.

**Confirmation checklist for a strong reversal (bull or bear, mirror-image lists)**: strong reversal bar (large body, small/no opposing tail); next 2–3 bars also strong same-direction bodies; spike runs 5–10 bars without more than a 1-bar pullback; first pullback comes only after 3+ bars and doesn't hit breakeven; the signal is a **second attempt** to reverse (second entry); the reversal began from a TCL overshoot; it reverses a significant swing point; the test-of-the-old-extreme leg lacks momentum (overlapping, counter-colored bars) or fails right at the moving average/old trend line.

**Stops**: typically placed one tick beyond the signal bar (or the entry bar once it closes and confirms). For a climax-bar fade, risk roughly the height of the climax bar itself, reducing size if that's much larger than normal. In a genuine always-in reversal, once price has moved ~2× the initial risk, move the stop to breakeven; thereafter trail below/above the most recent higher low/lower high.

**Scaling**: on the first (riskier, lower-probability) leg of a reversal, take partial profits at 1–2× risk and swing the remainder; add to the position on subsequent with-trend pullbacks once the reversal is confirmed (each addition itself managed mostly as a scalp, with some carried as swing). Second-entry setups are systematically preferred over first entries for anything countertrend, precisely because the *first* failed attempt clears out the weak hands.

**Why reversal trades are lower-probability / riskier than with-trend trades**: they fight inertia (the ~80% continuation-failure base rate), the "ideal" stop for a genuine trend reversal is beyond the most recent higher-low/lower-high — which is often far away — and no trader ever knows in advance whether a reversal will produce a full opposite trend or just a large trading range (both are traded identically, but a trading range favors scalping over swinging). This is why most individual reversal attempts carry only 40–50% probability even when the setup "looks good"; the trader's equation is rescued by reward being multiples of risk, not by high win rate.


## Trade Management & the Trader's Equation

Brooks' math: take a trade only if probability(success) × reward > probability(failure) × risk. If uncertain, assume 50%; if the setup looks good, assume ≥60% (this "60%" appears constantly as the default confidence level for a decent setup). Two broad reversal-trading postures: (a) **swing** for reward ≥2× risk at 50–60% probability, or (b) **scalp** for reward ≥ risk at 60–70% probability. Never scalp when risk > reward — that requires an unrealistic ~70–80% win rate most traders can't sustain ("the scalper's dilemma").

Concrete numeric pattern repeated across chapters: with a typical decent reversal setup, roughly 40% of trades hit a full swing target, ~30% get stopped out for a small loss, ~30% scratch for a small profit — net positive expectancy (~1.6 points/trade cited for the Emini) even though most individual trades don't reach the big target.

Never move a stop to breakeven too early — Brooks suggests waiting for meaningful cushion (roughly 60–80 cents in a stock, or a comparable multi-tick buffer) since "breakout tests" routinely run breakeven stops to the exact tick before the real move continues.


## Timing: When Reversals Happen

- The **first hour** (loosely defined, 15 min–2 hrs) is prime reversal territory: either the day's high or low forms in the first hour on ~90% of days; the very first bar is the extreme only ~20% of the time (up to 50%+ on a strong-trend-bar gap day). The day's high typically starts from some (often imperfect) **double top**; the low from an imperfect **double bottom** — the same fractal logic as any other reversal, just localized to the open.
- Opening-range **size** forecasts the day: a very small range (~25% of recent average) → likely trend-from-open day; a medium range (~⅓–½ of average) → likely breaks out, runs a measured move, gets tested, and — if the test reverses strongly back through the range and closes at the *opposite* side — becomes a reversal day (Brooks: "this is in fact the most common way that reversal days form"); a large opening range/spike → either a spike-and-channel trend day or (less often) a climactic reversal off a small final flag.
- **Gap opens**: treated as one giant invisible trend bar / breakout. Larger gaps raise trend-day odds (cited split for a large gap up: ~50% bull channel, ~20% trading range, ~30% reverses into a bear trend).
- Institutional order-imbalance mechanics explain most opening reversals: institutions with a large buy program actually *prefer* a gap-up open followed by a sharp sell-off into their target zone (a sell vacuum) so they can fill size at a good price while manufacturing a convincing "low of the day"; mirror logic (buy vacuum) explains sharp reversals down from a gap-down open.
- On **daily/weekly/monthly charts**, the same mechanics apply at magnets — especially **round numbers**, which act like measured-move targets: price is typically sucked 5–10% past a round number before pulling back to retest/penetrate it at least once.


## Common Mistakes Brooks Warns Against

- **Picking tops/bottoms too early.** "Too early is always worse than too late" — since most reversal attempts fail, an early countertrend entry is usually a loser, while a late with-trend entry in a real trend is usually still fine.
- **Countertrend scalping in a channel.** A channel "always looks like it's about to reverse" — beginners see a reasonable-looking reversal bar at every new extreme and lose on ~70% of these fades, with average losses exceeding average wins. Brooks: "You will not make consistent money until you stop trading countertrend scalps."
- **Fading a strong trend without a prior trend-line break.** "There are no reliable countertrend patterns" absent (1) a genuine trend-line break and (2) a strong signal bar on the subsequent test.
- **Trading tight trading ranges with stop entries.** "A tight trading range trumps everything" — it is literally the worst environment for stop-entry trades because institutions are doing the opposite (buying below bars, selling above them).
- **Confusing scalp and swing setups**, especially in the opening range — planning a swing but grabbing scalp-sized profit out of relief (or vice versa) reliably loses money; decide the plan before entering.
- **Watching smaller timeframes for countertrend signals.** A "reversal" on a 1-minute chart within a strong 5-minute trend is almost always just a with-trend flag; the correct trade is to place a with-trend entry exactly where the trapped 1-minute countertrend traders will be forced to cover.
- **Overreacting to news/pundits/volume.** News is most bullish at tops and most bearish at bottoms; volume is largely unreliable as a reversal signal on index futures/ETFs (somewhat more useful on individual daily stock charts, but even there requires *confirming bullish/bearish price action*, not volume alone — LEH vs. BSC in 2008 is Brooks' worked contrast).
- **Cherry-picking.** Skipping "ugly" but statistically sound setups while waiting for a perfect one reliably loses money over a large sample, because the best setups happen too fast to selectively catch.


## Key Rules of Thumb and Cited Probabilities

- ~80% of trend reversal attempts fail (become flags); ~80% of trading-range breakout attempts fail (price pulled back into the range); ~80% of head-and-shoulders/topping-continuation patterns resolve with the trend.
- A typical "good" setup ≈ 60% probability; "strong"/highest-confidence setups ≈ 70%; an ordinary reversal attempt (even a "reasonably good-looking" one) often carries only 40–50%.
- Major-trend-reversal setups with >60% success form only "a few times a week"; 50–60%-probability / ≥2×-reward reversal setups occur "just about every day in every market."
- First bar of the day = day's extreme ~20% of the time (up to 50%+ on a big-gap strong-trend-bar day); day's high or low forms in the first 5 bars ~50% of days, within the first hour or two ~90% of days.
- A pullback retracing ≥75% of the prior move should be treated as the start of a new opposite trend, not a pullback.
- Scalper's-target failures are named with specific tick/cent thresholds (5-, 9-, 17-tick failures in the Emini) and are themselves diagnostic of weakening momentum / incipient reversal.


## Brooks' Bottom Line on Reversals

Reversal trades exist because inertia is never absolute — every breakout eventually meets one that fails for good. But because failure is the *default* outcome for any countertrend attempt, Brooks' operating principle is: default to assuming continuation; treat every top as a potential bull flag and every bottom as a potential bear flag; require the Four-Point major-trend-reversal checklist (trend, break, tested extreme, consensus) or a clearly climactic/final-flag setup before acting countertrend; prefer second entries to first; size stops to the actual structure (usually beyond the signal bar, sometimes beyond the whole spike); and accept that even a well-qualified reversal setup is often only a 40–60% probability trade — rescued mathematically by reward that is genuinely, not hopefully, several times the risk.
