## 1. Core Philosophy

- **The market is a collection of institutions.** ~90%+ of volume is institutional; nearly all institutional strategies are net profitable over time (unprofitable ones go extinct), so every trade has a profitable trader on the other side. Price only reaches a level because institutions want to trade there — the market will not "run your stops" for spite, it tests a price only because institutions find it profitable to trade there.
- **Read every bar.** Brooks insists on reading price action bar-by-bar rather than relying on a handful of obvious setups a day — every bar carries information about who is in control.
- **No indicators needed** beyond a single 20-period exponential moving average (EMA). Indicators are mostly derivative of price and, in his words, obfuscate instead of elucidate. Volume, Fibonacci, and Elliott Wave are treated as marginal at best — Fibonacci retracements are not reliable enough to trade alone, and Elliott Wave is essentially useless to the vast majority of traders.
- **Ignore news and TV pundits.** The chart already reflects what smart institutional money thinks of the news; a chart reader does not need to know why, only what the market is doing.
- **Probabilistic, gray-fog thinking.** Nothing is ever certain. Brooks uses "likely / probably / usually" to mean at least 60% confident, and "unlikely" to mean at most 40%. Close is close enough — a pattern that reasonably resembles a textbook setup will likely behave like one.
- **The Trader's Equation:** take a trade only when P(success) times reward is meaningfully greater than P(failure) times risk. If uncertain, assume 50/50; if confident, assume 60/40. Reliable, universally exploitable trading rules cannot exist in a zero-sum game — only guidelines and probabilities.
- **Directional probability is ~50/50 most of the day** (the hallmark of a trading range); it briefly rises to 60-70%+ during the spike phase of a strong trend — that brief window is where real edge lives.
- **Market inertia** dominates: a trend tends to keep trending, a trading range tends to keep ranging. When a market is trending, most attempts to reverse fail; when it is in a trading range, most attempts to break out fail — roughly 70-80% failure rates cited repeatedly (see Section 8).


## 2. Terminology (Brooks' Precise Definitions)

- **Price action**: any change in price on any chart type or time frame — deliberately the broadest possible definition.
- **Trend bar** vs. **doji**: every bar is one or the other. A trend bar has a real body (close not equal to open) — one side controlled it. A doji has a tiny or no body — neither side controlled it; it is a one-bar trading range. Close is close enough — a near-doji behaves like a doji.
- **Trend / swing / leg / pullback**: four overlapping, fractal size-categories, largest to smallest. A trend is a series of price changes mostly up (bull) or down (bear); a chart usually shows only one or two major trends. A swing or leg is any smaller up/down move (terms used interchangeably once there are two or more on the chart). A pullback is a temporary countertrend move that does not retrace beyond the start of the trend/swing/leg it belongs to.
- **Always in**: if forced to hold a position at all times, always-in long/short is your current forced stance. Confidently knowing which way you would trade right now if forced = the market is always in that direction. Almost all always-in flips require a spike in the new direction before traders trust it. Rule: at any moment only two of {long, short, flat} are compatible with being a good trader — in a bull trend you should only be long or flat.
- **Spike**: one or more trend bars moving fast with little overlap — the market agreeing that the current price zone has no value, so it moves quickly to find a new one. A spike is simultaneously a breakout, a gap, and a climax.
- **Channel**: the lower-momentum, two-sided phase that follows a spike — a sloping trading range. Every trend alternates between spike phase (certainty, urgency) and channel phase (two-sided, "wall of worry").
- **Climax**: a move that went too far, too fast; ends at the first pause/pullback bar, not necessarily at a reversal. A climactic reversal requires both a climax and a trend bar breaking out the opposite way.
- **Trading range**: sideways two-sided action; the minimum is one bar whose range is largely overlapped by the prior bar. Usually just a pullback on a higher time frame chart.
- **Breakout**: current bar's high/low extends beyond a prior significant level (swing point, bar, trend line, channel line). Breakout pullback: a small 1-5 bar retracement shortly after a breakout, expected to resume. Failed breakout: the opposite expectation. Failed failure: a failure that itself fails and resumes the original breakout — a second, more reliable signal.
- **Signal bar / entry bar / setup bar**: a setup is any one-or-more-bar pattern that could justify an order. The bar right before the entry is retroactively labeled the signal bar once an order triggers; the triggering bar is the entry bar; the next bar is the follow-through bar.
- **Reversal bar**: a trend bar reversing the prior bar(s)' direction. Minimum: close beyond the open or midpoint. Best examples have: open near/beyond the prior close, a tail one-third to one-half the bar's height on the losing side, little overlap with priors, a strong (not doji) bar following it, and a close that reverses the closes/highs (or lows) of more than one prior bar.
- **Two-bar reversal**: two consecutive, roughly-equal, opposite-direction trend bars — present (at some time frame) in every reversal. If a would-be single reversal bar overlaps the prior bar more than about 75%, treat the pair as a two-bar reversal unit and use the combined extreme as your entry trigger (avoids one-tick bear/bull traps).
- **High 1/2/3/4, Low 1/2/3/4** (bar-counting / leg-counting system — Brooks' core alternative to Elliott Wave and "ABC"): in a bull-trend pullback, the first bar whose high exceeds the prior bar's high = High 1, ending leg 1. If the market keeps correcting instead of trending up, the next such bar is High 2 (this High 2 = the classic ABC correction: leg down = A, the bounce forming High 1 = B, the second leg down = C, and the breakout of C is the High 2 entry). A third leg = High 3 (usually a wedge bull flag); a fourth = High 4 (often really just a higher-time-frame High 2, or a hidden spike-and-channel). Mirror terms Low 1-4 apply to bear-trend/trading-range corrections. Each higher number is generally a weaker signal — except a 3-push (wedge) shape, which is specifically favored.
- **Wedge**: classically a 3-push move where each push extends further and the trend line plus trend channel line converge (rising/falling triangle). Any 3-push pattern trades like a wedge. Can be a pullback (wedge flag — with-trend, take the first signal) or a reversal pattern (wedge reversal — countertrend, take the second signal/pullback after the initial break).
- **Spike and channel**: a breakout (spike) followed by lower-momentum, two-sided channel — the single most common trend shape, present in some form on almost every chart at every scale.
- **Measuring gap / micro measuring gap**: when the pullback after a breakout does not overlap the breakout point (or, for a single strong trend bar, the bars immediately before and after it do not overlap) — a sign of strength that often anchors a measured-move projection.
- **Vacuum (buy/sell vacuum)**: strong traders on one side deliberately wait for price to reach a magnet before acting, so the market is sucked toward that level almost unopposed (one or more strong trend bars), then reverses hard once the waiting side finally engages.
- **Magnet**: any price level (measured-move target, trend line, prior trading range) that price is drawn toward because both sides see value there; a tight trading range itself acts as a magnet that pulls failed breakouts back in.
- **Barbwire**: a tight trading range of three or more largely-overlapping bars, one or more a doji, often with prominent tails.
- **Shrinking stairs**: a series of three or more trending swings where each new breakout extends by fewer ticks than the last — a waning-momentum warning.
- **20 (moving-average) gap bars**: 20 or more consecutive bars that have not touched the MA — an extremely strong trend signal; the eventual MA touch usually sets up a test of the trend's extreme.


## 3. Trend Types and Classification

**Spectrum framing:** price action exists on a spectrum from an extreme trend (nearly every tick the same direction) to an extreme trading range (every micro-up followed by a micro-down); the market rarely sits at either extreme for long, and most of the time is something in between. All trends contain smaller trading ranges and vice versa (fractal nesting) — even the 1987 and 2009 crashes were, in his framing, just pullbacks to the monthly bull trend line.

**Structural requirement for any trend:** a trend, at any size, must (1) first break a trend line from the prior trend or a support/resistance line from a prior trading range, AND (2) then show trending swings (consistently higher highs/lows, or lower highs/lows). Absent either, there is no trend.

**Life cycle of a trend:** SPIKE (steep, minimal overlap, small tails, brief, can be a single bar — the point where most traders agree always-in has flipped) leads to CHANNEL (pullbacks emerge, momentum decays, more overlap, more opposite-colored bars, can last far longer than expected), and the channel effectively becomes a pullback on a higher time frame, eventually breaking into an opposite-direction spike and a new trend. A trend generally loses strength as it matures: small pullbacks with strong with-trend signal bars early on give way to weak-looking pullback signal bars late (forcing traders to chase).

**Trend maturation / sentiment-transition sequence:** strong trend (dominant side buys/sells every dip, other side sits out) leads to the opposite side's "measure of excess" being reached and it starts fading the extreme, so the dominant side shifts from "buy the breakout" to "buy only the dip, take profit at new extremes" (a trading-range mindset creeps in), pullbacks deepen into real two-sided trading-range behavior, and a hard break of trend line plus MA flips the dominant side from "buy the dip" to "sell the rally" — reversal or a bigger correction follows. Traders and institutions transition gradually (bullish to neutral to bearish), not instantly, absent major news.

**"Always in" and the 3-outcome framework for a new swing extreme:** a new high in a bull trend produces one of three outcomes — (1) more buying/pressing longs, leading to a measured move (rare/most bullish); (2) profit-taking (by far the most common), leading to sideways drift, still-bullish players just waiting for a dip; (3) aggressive new shorting, leading to a hard reversal, expecting two or more legs and at least 10 bars down. Diagnostic: if price runs far enough past the new high for a scalp before any pullback, assume new buying; sideways action means profit-taking; a hard reversal means strong sellers took control.

**Two Legs (Brooks' structural alternative to Elliott Wave):** the market regularly tries to do something twice. Two failed attempts at the same thing usually mean the market then tries the opposite; a successful second attempt often extends the trend. Brooks reframes Elliott's Wave 3 as simply "the first major with-trend leg" (which itself often subdivides into two pushes), and Wave 5 as "two more pushes to test the extreme" after a pullback — an intentionally simpler, tradable substitute for Elliott theory, which he considers largely useless in practice.


## 4. Trend Lines, Channels, and Wedges

- **Trend line** (support in a bull trend / resistance in a bear trend) sets up with-trend entries on pullbacks to it. **Trend channel line (TCL)**, drawn on the opposite side of price from the trend line, is the primary tool for finding tradable countertrend fades — an overshoot-and-reversal of the TCL, especially on a second penetration.
- **Rule of Three Pushes:** bull/bear channels typically need, and often end at, a third push/leg. Traders generally do not seriously look for a channel's reversal until after the third leg, especially with a TCL overshoot and a strong reversal bar.
- **Vacuum mechanism at channel extremes:** as price nears the TCL, both profit-taking with-trend traders and new counter-trend traders hold back waiting for the line to be touched, creating a brief imbalance (hence the sharp trend bar into the line); once there, institutional counter-traders act aggressively while with-trend traders take quick profits, and price snaps back toward the trend line, where the mirror process repeats. This oscillation explains why channels persist instead of accelerating.
- **A channel is a sloping trading range.** Treat all bull channels as bear flags and all bear channels as bull flags, regardless of whether an opposite trend preceded them — eventually the dominant side stops buying/selling and a countertrend breakout follows.
- **Wedge mechanics:** a wedge shape emerges from an urgency asymmetry — the trend line's slope (where with-trend traders enter) becomes steeper than the TCL's slope (where they exit), because both sides keep getting more eager on smaller and smaller retracements. When a new push fails to reach the newly-projected steeper extreme, that is the wedge reversal signal. If you find yourself continually redrawing ever-steeper channel lines because price keeps blowing through them, that is usually a sign you are on the wrong side of the market.
- **Micro channel**: an extremely tight channel (2-10 bars) with zero or only tiny pullbacks — a spike on a smaller/other time frame. Micro trend lines should only be used for with-trend setups — never trade a countertrend break of a micro trend line inside a larger trend; instead anticipate its failure and buy/sell exactly where those countertrend traders get stopped out.
- **Measured move (core recurring target rule):** after any successful breakout of a channel, trading range, or double top/bottom, the initial target equals the height of the pattern projected from the breakout point. Works for horizontal patterns and sloping channels alike (measure the vertical distance between the two channel lines and project it). Also usable as "leg 1 equals leg 2" (the channel should travel about as far as the spike that started it).
- **Horizontal lines / swing points**: on trading-range days, treat old swing highs/lows as fade zones (expect failed breakouts, "higher high"/"lower low" reversal setups); a second, more extreme high/low is an even better fade (second-signal reliability). On trend days, use the same old levels only for with-trend breakout-pullback entries.


## 5. Bar-by-Bar Signal Mechanics

- **Every bar is a signal bar for both directions** — stop-order buyers above it, stop-order sellers below it, plus fade traders on limits at the opposite extremes. It only becomes an actual signal bar retroactively once a trade triggers on the next bar.
- **Core asymmetry rule:** countertrend/reversal entries need much stronger signal bars than with-trend pullback entries, because most countertrend trades fail. The very strongest trends usually have terrible-looking signal bars, but the trades are still excellent. Conversely, an obviously perfect setup usually means the trend is weak (too many traders would take it, so it just becomes the final flag before a bigger correction).
- **Second entries beat first entries** — "good fill, bad trade" is the maxim: if a second attempt lets you in at a better price than the first, be suspicious it is a trap; legitimate second entries are usually at the same price or worse (you pay for the extra confirmation).
- **Overlap invalidates a reversal bar**: if a would-be reversal bar's midpoint does not clear the prior bar's extreme (excessive overlap), it is probably just part of a small trading range, not a real reversal — do not trade it, especially countertrend. A big reversal bar in a three-or-more-bar flag near the MA is a bull/bear trap roughly 60% of the time.
- **Doji-after-signal-bar rule:** if the bar right after your signal bar is a doji/inside bar, the market lacks urgency — skip the trade unless the setup is otherwise very strong. A doji within a few bars of a reversal has roughly a 50% chance of being violated — do not move a stop to just below it expecting safety.
- **Never trade the bar before it closes.** Bars can completely flip their appearance in the final seconds (a great reversal bar can close on the opposite extreme). Only exception: with-trend entries in an already-strong trend, where being wrong early costs little.
- **Outside bars** are complicated (both sides had control at some point) — generally do not trade their own breakout directly; wait for the bar to close, or treat them as valid entries only when the prior bar was already a decent signal bar (especially as a second entry after a trend-line break/TCL overshoot).


## 6. The Common Trend-Day Patterns (Setup Catalog)

Brooks explicitly says the names do not matter — what matters is recognizing the recurring price-action shape early enough to commit to swinging with-trend size. Most days show features of more than one pattern simultaneously.

**General operating rules while any of these patterns is in play:** take every with-trend entry no matter how weak it looks; take countertrend entries only after an actual trend-line break and a good reversal bar, and only if you are still catching every with-trend signal; scalp all countertrend trades in full (never swing them); cap yourself at two or three countertrend trades a day — more means you are mis-prioritizing. The stronger the trend, the more you should swing with it and the less you should scalp against it.

**Opening range as a forecast tool:** a small opening range signals an expected breakout (breakout-mode day). An opening range about half the recent average daily range often means the breakout produces a Trending Trading Range Day. A large opening range usually points to a Spike and Channel day.

### a) Spike and Channel Trend
The most common trend type — some form appears almost every day. Spike (one or more trend bars, clear always-in flip, "breakaway gap") is followed by a pullback (one to about 20-plus bars), then channel resumption, usually weaker/flatter/more overlapping than the spike. The channel rarely extends further with-trend (and if it does, that breakout usually fails within about five bars); it eventually breaks against the trend (do not enter on that breakout itself — wait for its pullback) and typically corrects back to test the start of the channel (a test of the "gap" the spike created), often forming a double bottom/top flag there before bouncing. The channel often ends on the third push at a measured-move target (the spike's own height, or "leg 1 equals leg 2"). Consecutive climaxes (a pause, then a second full spike instead of a mellow channel) are a variant, and typically produce a larger, 10-plus-bar, two-legged correction than a single climax would.

### b) Trending Trading Range Day
Opening range about a third to a half of the recent average range, followed by a breakout after one to two hours that forms another trading range (not a clean channel); often a pullback tests the earlier range. About twice as common as spike-and-channel days. Because there are multiple ranges, trade both directions — near the measured-move target, shift from trend-style to range-style trading (take profits into strength, do not trail stops on weakness). Most Reversal Days begin life as trending trading range days, so once you spot this pattern, stay alert for a late reversal swing that could cover the whole day's range.

### c) Trend from the Open / Small Pullback Trend
The day's extreme (low of a bull day, high of a bear day) forms in the first few bars — usually the strongest pattern, but occurs on only about 20% of days (so simply betting bar 1 is the extreme is low-probability on its own). A large gap becomes a trend-from-open day in the gap's direction roughly 60% of the time (40% the other way) — the bigger the gap, the more likely both a trend day and a trend in the gap's direction. The Small Pullback sub-variant (rarer, once or twice a month) is the single strongest trend form: pullbacks stay tiny (10-30% of the average daily range) all day, with one larger (about 150-200% of the earlier pullbacks) pullback typically appearing after 11 a.m. before the trend resumes into the close. With-trend setups here are 70%-plus reliable even though they look like coin-flips to a beginner — countertrend noise bars are a deliberate-feeling feature that keeps weaker traders hunting for reversals that never pay off.

### d) Reversal Day
Trends one way, then reverses and trends the opposite way into the close; usually begins life as a trending trading range day. A late, strong reversal (in the final couple of hours) often has follow-through into the next day, or several days. Early warning: a "pullback" that begins with an unusually strong countertrend spike bar (not a typical weak pullback) may be the seed of a full reversal, not just a flag. Self-diagnostic: if you keep losing on with-trend scalps in the old direction, that string of losses is itself a sign the trend has flipped.

### e) Trend Resumption Day
Strong trend in the first hour, followed by an extended, often tight trading range lasting hours (lulls traders into complacency), and then the trend resumes in the final hour or two, with the second leg often about the same size as the first. Frequently a late false breakout attempts to reverse the trend (a trap, especially if the range was unusually tight) right before the real breakout resumes the original direction. Mechanism: during the long consolidation both sides keep scaling in until many hit max size; once the real breakout starts, the losing side cannot add anymore and is forced to capitulate, adding fuel. Can span multiple days (a choppy 5-minute chart can be a clean two-leg move on a 60-minute chart).

### f) Stairs (Broad Channel Trend)
A trending-trading-range variant with three or more trading ranges and broad swings but still trending highs/lows overall — a large-scale sloping trading range. Because swings are broad, both directions are tradeable, but with-trend swings should still be favored. Nearly every breakout gets a pullback (a "breakout test") that goes back beyond the breakout point but stays short of the prior extreme. Shrinking stairs (each breakout smaller than the last) signals waning momentum and often precedes a two-legged reversal.


## 7. Trade Management

- **Stop placement:** typically one tick beyond the signal bar's far extreme; in a fast/strong spike, size down so total dollar risk matches your normal trade (since the stop must sit beyond the whole spike).
- **Scalp vs. swing:** a scalp targets a small, quick profit (often smaller than the risk) and therefore statistically needs a very high win rate (about 70%-plus) to be worthwhile — assume you will never be that good, as a rule for most traders. A swing trade targets reward at least equal to risk and needs only about 50-60% win rate to be profitable long-term; Brooks recommends beginners default to swing trading.
- **Concrete profit-taking plan (from an interviewed successful trader):** buy early in the trend, determine initial risk (R), take half the position off at 2R profit, hold the rest for a clear reversal signal (or exit near the close if none appears), and trail the stop below each new higher low after every new high.
- **Trailing stops:** after every new trend extreme, move the stop to just beyond the most recent pullback extreme (not tighter). Do not jump to breakeven prematurely — a pullback very often revisits (and slightly undercuts) the original entry before the trend makes a new extreme; premature breakeven stops are a named, common way traders get shaken out of otherwise-winning trades.
- **Scaling in:** pre-plan each incremental order's size so total risk stays equal to one normal trade's risk. Limit-order scaling into a trend (buying dips at fixed tick/point intervals, or at the moving average after 20-plus bars without touching it) is a standard institutional-style technique; avoid scaling into a countertrend position during the second half of the trading day (insufficient time to recover if wrong).
- **Fixed pullback-depth tactic:** note the day's biggest pullback so far and place limit entries at a similar distance from new extremes (for example, if the biggest pullback has been 8 ticks, buy 5-7 ticks below a new high).
- **Late/missed entries:** if you would still be holding the swing portion of a trade you missed, enter now at market with the same size you would currently hold and the same stop level you would currently have — mathematically identical risk to having held from the start; treat it as real risk, not house money.


## 8. Key Rules of Thumb and Cited Probabilities

- About 50/50 directional probability most of the trading day (hallmark of a trading range); briefly 60-80% during a strong trend's spike phase.
- About 80% of attempts to reverse an established trend fail (become flags); symmetrically, about 80% of breakout attempts out of a trading range fail.
- The market is always trying to reverse, but about 80% of those reversal attempts fail and become with-trend flags.
- A pullback that retraces 75% or more of the prior move has a substantially reduced chance of the trend resuming to a new extreme — better treated as a possible new trend in the opposite direction.
- In roughly a third of cases, a spike-and-trading-range combo breaks out the "wrong" way (against the original trend).
- On a large gap opening, a trend-from-open day follows the gap's direction about 60% of the time.
- Even the best, most reliable patterns still fail roughly 40% of the time — always have a plan for the opposite outcome.
- A trend line tested many times without breaking usually either finally breaks through (most common) or, less often, the market accelerates away from it as counter-side traders give up.


## 9. Common Mistakes Brooks Warns Against

- **Fading a strong trend** because it looks overdone — this is the single most repeated warning in the book. The market can sustain unsustainable behavior far longer than a trader can sustain their account.
- **Countertrend scalping** to catch a small pullback in a strong trend — explicitly called usually a mistake.
- **Scalping with risk equal to or greater than reward** without the (rare) about-70%-plus win rate required to make that profitable.
- **Watching a smaller time frame simultaneously** with your trading chart (for example, 1-minute while trading the 5-minute) — leads to cherry-picking trades and getting stopped out by noise the primary chart would not show.
- **Trading the news or TV pundits** — described at length as actively harmful, since pundits' time frames, incentives, and track records are irrelevant to a day trader, and the chart already reflects informed reaction.
- **Switching your plan mid-trade** — for example, a swing trader scalping out early after recent losses (missing the big win needed to offset a sub-50% swing win rate), or a scalper refusing to take a full profit hoping for a repeat big move, and giving it back as a loss.
- **Moving a stop to breakeven too early** in a legitimately strong trend, getting shaken out right before a new extreme.
- **Over-relying on indicators/candle-pattern taxonomies** (hammer, harami, hanging man, etc.) — Brooks calls the added naming meaningless and a distraction from the only real question: is the market trending or not, and which side is in control of this bar.
- **Entering on an unclosed bar** to save a tick or two of risk — the bar can completely reverse its appearance in the final seconds.
- **Martingale-style position doubling after losses** — mathematically sound in theory but practically impossible once position sizes explode after several losses.
- **Gambling disguised as trading** — deviating from your rules out of hope/anxiety after losses, rather than logic, is explicitly labeled a form of gambling, not trading.


## 10. Summary of Overall Approach

Brooks' method is: (1) classify every bar as trend or doji, and every stretch of the chart as trending or ranging; (2) default hard to trading with the always-in direction, treating countertrend setups mainly as opportunities to read exactly where the other side gets trapped and trade against them; (3) use trend lines for with-trend entries and trend channel lines for the rare, carefully-filtered countertrend fades; (4) expect trends to unfold as alternating spike (urgency, momentum) and channel (worry, two-sidedness) phases, generally in two-legged pushes, usually pausing or reversing near a measured-move target or the third push; (5) size and manage trades according to an explicit trader's equation, favoring swing trades over scalps for most traders; and (6) accept that nothing is ever certain — every read is a probability estimate in the gray fog, and discipline (sticking to a plan despite scary-looking with-trend setups and tempting-looking countertrend ones) is what separates consistently profitable traders from the rest.
