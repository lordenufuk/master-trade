## Overall Philosophy: How Brooks Thinks About Non-Trending Markets

- **The market is fractal.** Every pattern on a chart is a smaller version of a pattern on a higher time frame chart and a larger version of a pattern on a lower time frame chart. Strip the price/time labels off a chart and you usually cannot tell if it's a 3-minute, 60-minute, or monthly chart.
- **Every bar is a test.** "The market is always trying to break out, and then the market tries to make every breakout fail." Every trend bar is simultaneously a breakout, a spike, a gap, and a climax. The single most important recurring decision a trader makes, bar after bar, is: *will there be more buyers or sellers above/below this bar* — i.e., will this breakout succeed or fail?
- **The market gravitates toward uncertainty.** For most of the day the directional probability of an equidistant move (the odds price moves X ticks up before X ticks down, versus the reverse) hovers near 50%. This 50/50 condition is the definition of a trading range. Brooks' rule of thumb: "whenever you are uncertain, assume the market is in a trading range."
- **Trend and range are mirror-image failure regimes.** In a trend, most attempts to *reverse* the trend fail (roughly 80%). In a trading range, most attempts to *break out* of the range fail (roughly 80%). This single asymmetry — not a coin flip — is why trading ranges are fought by fading extremes rather than by chasing breakouts, and why trends are ridden by buying/selling pullbacks rather than by picking tops and bottoms.
- **Institutions, not individuals, move the market.** ~90%+ of volume is institutional; ~70% of volume is now algorithmic/HFT. Traditional institutions determine the overall *direction* and *target* (based on fundamentals); statistically-driven programs determine the *path* to get there. A price only trades where at least one institution wants to buy and another wants to sell — "the market will not run your stops" out of malice; a stop is only hit because some institution found that exact price profitable to trade at.
- **Everything reduces to the trader's equation**: probability(success) × reward must exceed probability(failure) × risk. Edges are always small and fleeting in an efficient, zero-sum market filled with smart, well-capitalized counterparties, so a setup that looks too good (high probability + big reward + tiny risk, simultaneously) is being misread, not a real gift.
- **Ignore news and indicators.** The chart already reflects every institution's interpretation of the news; a single 20-bar EMA is the only "indicator" Brooks uses. Divergence trades, Fibonacci levels, and band touches are treated as weak, secondary confirmations at best — never a reason on their own to fade a strong trend.


## Terminology

Brooks' glossary is large; the terms below are the ones load-bearing for range trading specifically.

- **Trading range** — his core, deliberately broad definition: *"The minimum requirement is a single bar with a range that is largely overlapped by the bar before it."* It is sideways movement where neither bulls nor bears are in control (though one side is often somewhat stronger). It is usually an extended pullback in a trend that has lasted long enough to lose its short-term predictive power — the market has had repeated failed breakout attempts in both directions, and it will usually (but not always) eventually break out in the direction of the larger trend. Every trading range is a pullback on some higher time frame chart, and every pullback is a small trading range on the current chart.
- **Tight trading range** — a range of 2+ bars with heavy overlap where most reversals are too small to trade profitably with stop entries; bulls and bears are in tight balance. Rule of thumb sizing on the 5-minute Emini (with a 10–15 point average daily range): a range of ~3 points or less is tight; 4–5 points can behave tight if the bars are unusually large.
- **Barbwire** — Brooks' specific coinage: *"A trading range of three or more bars that largely overlap and one or more is a doji."* It is a type of tight trading range, typically with prominent tails and often relatively large bars (meaning more emotion, not less). Rule of thumb for "largely overlap": look at the middle of any three consecutive candidate bars — if more than half its height sits inside the range of both the bar before and after it, it's barbwire.
- **Breakout mode** — a setup (single bar, ii/iii, ioi, small trading range, triangle) where a breakout in either direction "should" have follow-through; the market is poised to move but hasn't shown which way yet.
- **Breakout / breakout point / breakout test / breakout pullback / breakout gap** — a breakout is any move beyond a prior point of significance (a bar's high/low, a swing point, a trend line/channel line). The breakout point is the level broken. A breakout pullback is a small (1–5 bar) retracement shortly after a breakout that is expected to be followed by resumption. A breakout test is a breakout pullback that comes back close to (or slightly through) the original breakout/entry level, testing whether traders will defend it again. The breakout gap is the space between the breakout point and the pullback/test.
- **Failed failure** — a failure that itself fails and resumes in the original breakout direction; effectively a second, more reliable signal in the direction of the original breakout.
- **Five-tick failure** — an Emini trade that reaches five ticks beyond the signal bar (just short of a typical six-tick move needed for a four-tick net scalp) and then reverses; often the seed of a tradable move in the opposite direction.
- **High 1 / High 2 / High 3 / High 4 (and mirror Low 1–4)** — Brooks' core "bar counting" vocabulary. In an up-move that is correcting sideways or down, the first bar whose high exceeds the prior bar's high ends leg 1 of the correction (a High 1 entry). If price continues correcting and a second such bar occurs (with at least a minor trend-line break between the two), that's a High 2 (a classic "ABC" or two-legged pullback: leg-down A, minor bounce B, leg-down C, then the High-2 breakout). A High 3 is a three-push pullback (functionally a wedge bull flag). A High 4 is four pushes down, often just a higher-time-frame High 2 whose two legs each subdivided. Mirror Low 1–4 in down-moves. **Critically, these setups mean opposite things in a trend versus a range** (see Setups, below).
- **ABC correction** — Brooks' preferred plain-English label for a two-legged pullback (leg A down, bounce B, leg C down to a slightly different extreme), used instead of Elliott-style "AB=CD" labeling, which he finds needlessly confusing.
- **Double top / double bottom, double top bear flag / double bottom bull flag** — a double top is simply the current bar's high approximately matching a prior swing high (any distance back); a double bottom is the mirror. When this happens as a pause *within* an existing trend (not as a reversal at a major extreme), it is a **double top bear flag** (continuation lower-high pattern in a bear trend) or **double bottom bull flag** (continuation higher-low pattern in a bull trend) — every double bottom is, by definition, a High-2 buy setup, and every double top is a Low-2 sell setup.
- **Wedge / wedge flag / wedge reversal** — a wedge is a three-push (or more) pattern where the trend line and trend channel line converge, sloping *against* the prevailing direction (down-sloping in an up-move, up-sloping in a down-move). As a **wedge flag** (with-trend pullback) it's traded on the first breakout signal. As a **wedge reversal** (countertrend, ending a trend) it needs to be larger (Brooks suggests at least ~20 bars with a clear channel) and is safer traded on the *second* signal/breakout pullback. The exact shape rarely matters — what matters is that there are three-plus pushes, which is enough to trap countertrend traders who mistake the third push for a new trend.
- **Triangle** — any trading range with a minimum of three pushes up or down. A contracting triangle sits between two converging trend lines; an expanding triangle (e.g., an "oo," outside-bar-followed-by-larger-outside-bar) sits between two diverging trend-channel lines. Ascending/descending/symmetrical triangles are all traded the same way (they're all just trading ranges). A wedge is simply a triangle that is clearly sloped rather than roughly horizontal — the label is about degree of slope only, not different mechanics.
- **Ledge** — a small trading range whose bottom is formed by two or more bars with identical lows (bull ledge) or whose top is formed by bars with identical highs (bear ledge).
- **ii / iii / ioi / oo** — consecutive-inside-bar patterns (2 or 3 inside bars) and inside-outside-inside / outside-outside patterns; all are breakout-mode setups, typically small triangles on a lower time frame chart.
- **Vacuum (buy vacuum / sell vacuum)** — occurs when the side that expects a reversal at some nearby magnet deliberately *steps aside* rather than trading early, waiting for a better price at the exact level; the resulting one-sided imbalance "sucks" price quickly to the magnet (often via one or two large trend bars with small tails, which look like a strong breakout), at which point the waiting side floods in and reverses price immediately. This is the mechanical explanation for why strong-looking breakout bars so often appear right before a reversal at range extremes.
- **Magnet / support / resistance** — any price level with enough of a directional-probability edge (rule of thumb: needs roughly a 60%+ implied edge to be tradable; a 52–53% edge is HFT-only scalping territory, not "support" in a practical sense). Support/resistance sources include trend lines, trend channel lines, any moving average, measured-move targets, prior swing highs/lows, entry/signal-bar extremes, yesterday's OHLC, gaps (including moving-average gaps), earlier trading ranges/barbwire (both the extremes *and* the middle get retested), round numbers, and the opposite extreme of a large trend bar.
- **Directional probability (of an equidistant move)** — the probability price moves X ticks in one direction before it moves X ticks in the other. Roughly 50% mid-range; rises to 60–70%+ in a genuine spike/trend or near a range extreme, then "overshoots" back the other way once price is clearly too far — mean-reversion of *probability*, not just of price.
- **Trader's equation** — probability(success) × reward > probability(failure) × risk. If uncertain, default to assuming 50/50; if the setup "looks good," default to assuming 60/40.
- **Always in** — the position (long or short) you would be forced to hold if you had to be in the market at all times. Confidence in a new always-in direction almost always requires seeing a real spike in that direction first.
- **Climax** — a move that has gone too far, too fast, and reverses — usually into a trading range, only occasionally into an opposite trend.
- **Second entry / second signal** — a repeat of the same setup logic within a few bars of the first; generally more reliable than the first attempt, because it means the first failed attempt trapped traders who now provide fuel (forced covering/liquidation) for the second.
- **Shrinking stairs** — a series of three or more trend swings where each new breakout extreme is reached by fewer ticks than the last, signaling waning momentum (often a precursor to a range or reversal).
- **Dueling lines** — the special case of a pullback ending right where a sloping trend line and a sloping trend channel line intersect (or where a sloping line and a horizontal support/resistance level intersect); two independent "lines of resistance" converging increases confidence in the reversal.


## Range Types

Brooks does not present a rigid taxonomy of range "types" so much as a spectrum defined by **tightness**, **slope**, and **context** (what came before the range, and what the higher time frame trend is doing):

1. **Ordinary trading range** — any area of clearly two-sided trading, horizontal or gently sloped, large enough that fading the extremes with a stop entry can produce a genuine scalper's profit (or more). Directional probability at the extremes is meaningfully above 50% (Brooks often cites ~60%, sometimes 70–80% if the range follows a strong prior trend and no clear top/bottom has yet formed).
2. **Tight trading range** — heavy overlap, many dojis, prominent tails, breakout probability capped at roughly 51–55% in the trend's favor (never much higher — "or else the market would not have formed the tight trading range" in the first place). There usually isn't enough room even if you're directionally right, so stop entries lose money here as a rule.
3. **Barbwire** — the most extreme/hostile form of tight trading range (3+ heavily overlapping bars, one or more dojis, often large bodies and tails). Brooks: *"Until you are a strong trader, don't touch barbwire or you will be hurt."*
4. **Trending trading range(s)** — two or more distinct trading ranges on the same chart separated by a breakout; the day (or move) is best understood as an upper range and a lower range (or several), with price cycling between them, roughly the height of the first range projected as a measured move for the second. A **trending trading range day** intraday is one where the opening range is small (well under the average daily range) and, once it finally breaks, tends to form a second range of similar height rather than a clean trend day.
5. **Sloping / channel-as-range** — any channel (a rising bull channel, a falling bear channel) is technically a trading range because genuine two-sided trading is occurring within it, even though the market label is "trend." A channel is literally a chain of small trading ranges, each followed by a small breakout into the next small range; as the channel matures the swings get larger until the channel converts into a recognizable trading range.
6. **Triangle / wedge as range** — a trading range with 3+ clean pushes; by definition it's in "breakout mode" — a break is considered imminent, though its direction and success are not predetermined. Because 3+ pushes represent more countertrend pressure than a simple one- or two-leg pullback, triangles/wedges skew toward appearing *late* in trends/swings and often become **final flags**.
7. **Range as reversal setup vs. range as continuation** — the great majority of ranges (~80%, mirroring the general breakout-failure rate) are continuation patterns that eventually resume the trend that preceded them; a minority genuinely mark a reversal. Classic "reversal" chart patterns — double tops/bottoms, head-and-shoulders tops/bottoms — are, in Brooks' reframing, *mostly* trading ranges that behave like continuation flags, not the textbook reversal signals they're commonly taught as. An H&S top forming *within a bull trend* is really a triangle/wedge bull flag (three down-legs after left shoulder, head, right shoulder) that should break out *above* the right shoulder, not below the neckline; the same H&S shape forming as a bear-flag range *within a bear trend* is the one that legitimately tends to break the neckline down. **Always ask "is this pattern with or against the larger trend" before deciding which side of the range you favor for the eventual breakout.**


## Setups

### The core range-trading motto

"**Buy low, sell high, and scalp.**" In a range, every rally functions like a bear flag (fade it: sell above/below bars, above/below any resistance) and every sell-off functions like a bull flag (fade it: buy above/below bars, above/below any support). Trade for scalps, not swings, unless a setup is unusually strong and at a genuine range extreme following a strong prior trend, in which case swinging part of the position is acceptable.

### Context-dependent High/Low setups (the single most important range-specific rule)

A **High 2** (or Low 2) means the *opposite* thing depending on where it sits:
- In a bull **trend**, a High 2 near/above the moving average is a *continuation* buy — safe to buy near the top of the move.
- In a **trading range**, a High 2 near the *top* of the range is a *reversal* bet — buying it is risky. It is often better to *short* at or above a High-2 signal bar inside a range, expecting it to fail, because bears rest their limit shorts above bar highs (not below), creating a local vacuum that lets price poke through and trigger the breakout stop before the wall of resting shorts reverses it immediately.
- The exact mirror applies to Low 2s: risky to short near the range bottom; better to fade (buy), expecting a failed breakdown/double bottom.

A **High 1 / Low 1** long/short is only reliable in the **spike phase of a confirmed, strong trend** — never merely a spike within a range, and never right after a significant buy/sell climax. A High-1-looking spike into the top of a range is usually just a vacuum test of resistance, not the start of a trend — treat it as a fade/short opportunity, not a buy. Taking every High 1/Low 1 on hope, regardless of context, is called out explicitly as "one of the most common mistakes" traders make.

Do not mix directions against the prevailing higher-time-frame trend: in a clear bull trend, don't look for Low 1/Low 2 shorts (and vice versa in a bear trend) — those patterns are only valid setups in a trading range or the opposite trend.

### Named entries within a range

**Stop-entry setups** (favored for less experienced traders — you enter with the market already moving your way):
1. Buy a High 2 near the bottom of the range (often a second attempt up — a double bottom).
2. Sell a Low 2 near the top of the range (often a second attempt down — a double top).
3. Buy at the bottom, especially a second entry after a break *above* a bear trend line.
4. Sell at the top, especially a second entry after a break *below* a bull trend line.
5. Buy a wedge bull flag near the bottom / sell a wedge bear flag near the top.
6. Buy a bull reversal bar or reversal pattern (e.g., final flag) after a break below a swing low at the range bottom; mirror for shorts at the top.
7. Buy a breakout pullback after an upside breakout near the bottom of the range; mirror for shorts at the top.

**Limit-order / countertrend-entry setups** (experienced traders only — price is still moving against you when the order fills):
1. Buy a bear spike at market or on a limit at/below the prior swing low at the range bottom (needs a wider stop; fast-moving, hard for most traders); mirror for selling a bull spike at the top.
2. Buy at the close of, or below the low of, a large bear trend bar near the bottom (treat it as an exhaustive sell climax); mirror at the top with a large bull trend bar.
3. Buy at or below a *weak* Low 1/Low 2 signal bar at the bottom (fade the weak breakdown attempt); mirror shorting a weak High 1/High 2 at the top.
4. Buy a bear close right at the start of what becomes a strong bull swing (rare, obvious only in hindsight, hard to execute live); mirror for shorts.

### Bar-counting mechanics inside a range

If a High 2 (or Low 2) fails, the market usually tries a High 3 (wedge) next, or a High 4. Judge which by post-failure momentum: if momentum stays weak/similar after the failure, expect the wedge to succeed at the third push (buy High 3 / short Low 3). If a strong opposite trend bar appears right after the failure, expect at least two more legs and wait for High 4/Low 4 instead. The exact number (3 vs. 4) is secondary — what matters functionally is that it behaves like a wedge (3+ pushes). Once a High 2/Low 2 setup fails and reverses again ("failed failure"), it's a second, more reliable signal in the *original* breakout's direction.

**Warning against over-trading bar counts in choppy ranges**: High 1/2 and Low 1/2 setups appear constantly inside a tight range (sometimes several within 10 bars) precisely because neither side is in control. Brooks: *"if you take every high 1, 2, 3, and 4 and also short every low 1, 2, 3, and 4, over the course of an hour you will lose all of the money that you made in the past week."* Most traders should step aside entirely rather than mechanically bar-count trade a tight/choppy range.

### Triangle and wedge-specific setups

A failed triangle/wedge breakout typically leads to a test of the *opposite* side of the pattern (treat it like any channel). A genuine breakout targets an approximate measured move equal to the height of the triangle; a move well beyond that suggests a real new trend, not just a triangle resolution. Expanding triangles frequently chain: an expanding-triangle top that fails can become the start of an expanding-triangle bottom, and vice versa — reversal and continuation labels can swap after the fact.

### "Need two reasons to take a trade"

A simple operating rule Brooks gives explicitly: require at least two independent reasons before entering (e.g., a good signal bar *and* a moving-average pullback *and* a trend-line test *and* a High-2/Low-2 pattern — any two or more of these stacking is enough). Exceptions requiring only *one* reason: any pullback in a confirmed strong trend (unless it follows a climax or final-flag reversal), a trend-line/trend-channel-line overshoot with a genuinely good reversal bar, or any *second* entry (the first entry itself counts as reason one).


## Trade Management

### Scalp vs. swing, and why ranges force scalping

A **scalper** targets a reward roughly equal to risk and needs a high win rate (Brooks: realistically ~60%+, ideally 70%+, since commissions and slippage erode anything smaller). A **swing trader** targets a reward at least twice the risk and can be profitable with a win rate as low as 40–50%. This is not a stylistic accident: it follows directly from the trader's equation being zero-sum — a scalper's high-probability trade logically requires someone on the other side accepting a lower-probability, higher-reward trade, and vice versa.

**The trading-range dilemma**, stated by Brooks directly: *"Since most breakouts fail, your profit goals are limited, which means that you are scalping. However, when you reduce your reward yet keep your risk the same, you need a much higher probability of success to satisfy the trader's equation... The dilemma is that you have to scalp, but scalping requires a very high probability of success, and that high degree of certainty cannot exist for long in a trading range, where by definition uncertainty prevails."* This is his explicit justification for why range trading is structurally harder, risk/reward-wise, than trend trading, and for why "trade less" is the correct prescription in choppy conditions.

Practical fixes for the dilemma:
- Enter via **limit orders at or beyond the extreme** (e.g., short a limit at the high of a weak High-2 signal bar) rather than stop-entering on the breakout — you get a better price and effectively invert the standard with-trend stop-entry logic.
- Trade a **smaller initial size and scale in** if the market goes further against you before reversing (see Scaling, below).
- Accept smaller first-scale-out targets but **tighten stops asymmetrically** once partial profit is banked, letting the reward-to-risk of the remaining piece improve even as its win probability is modest.

### Sizing risk and reward to the range

Concrete Emini-era example (10–15 point average daily range): shorting a strong second-entry bear signal at the top of a range that's ≥6 points tall gives roughly a 60% chance of a 2-point move — a minimally acceptable trade (matching a 2-point stop). If the range is only 3–4 points tall (a "relatively tight" range), stop entries are generally a *losing* strategy regardless of read quality; only very experienced traders should trade it at all, mostly with limit orders, and never by buying into a rally or selling into a sell-off (a classic beginner mistake that guarantees getting caught on the eventual real breakout).

### Stops

Two stop types: **money-management stops** (a fixed number of ticks/dollars) and **price-action stops** (beyond a specific bar/level). Pick one default method for consistency. Standard lifecycle: initial stop = one tick beyond the signal bar; tighten to one tick beyond the *entry* bar once it closes, *if* the entry bar is itself a real trend bar (not a doji); once the trade is working, trail the stop below/above the most recent swing point after each new extreme; once roughly halfway to the profit target, many traders move the stop to breakeven. Do **not** tighten a stop immediately after a doji entry bar — a doji is a one-bar trading range, and treating its edge as a hard reversal signal is statistically premature.

**Context-dependent stop discipline**: if a long's entry/signal bar reverses badly, whether to hold the normal stop or bail immediately depends on the larger trend. In a strong bull trend, keep the normal stop (a bad-looking bear entry bar is probably just noise). In a strong bear trend, if a countertrend long you took gets a bad reversal on the entry bar, exit immediately (and consider reversing to short), because most bull-reversal attempts inside a bear trend simply become bear flags.

Deliberately widened stops (with reduced size) are appropriate for: entering during a strong multi-bar spike (the "true" stop is far, at the spike's start — most traders should instead wait for a smaller-stop pullback entry); countertrend buys against a genuine climactic extreme (e.g., a third consecutive sell climax) — risk roughly the height of that climax bar, expecting a sharp reflex move, expert-only; and deliberate range-extreme scale-in fading (see below).

### Profit targets

A profit target is the mirror of a stop and should likewise sit as a working order, not just "in your head," to protect you from riding a winner back to breakeven or a loss. A common scale-out ladder for a strong swing: exit ~50% at 2× initial risk, another ~25% at 3× initial risk, trail the remainder with a stop, exit the final piece on an opposing signal or at the close. The simplest fallback for a trader uncomfortable scaling: exit 100% at 2× risk.

**"Reward often equals risk"** is an empirical pattern Brooks flags repeatedly: once you discover through a pullback how large a stop was actually needed to survive it (e.g., a trade survived an 11-tick adverse excursion), that same distance often becomes a realistic size for the eventual favorable move — track the day's largest realized "needed stop" and reuse it as the day's standard stop/target going forward (the first trade of the day often sets this benchmark).

### Scaling into and out of trades

Scaling into a **losing** position is appropriate mainly in two contexts: (a) a pullback within a strong, still-intact higher-time-frame trend, or (b) fading the extremes of a trading range. It is explicitly *not* recommended for picking a top/bottom on a pure reversal bet — "usually better to exit if the market goes against you and look for a second entry" instead. The "never add to a losing position" adage is reframed: it is really a warning against martingale-style averaging down on a *bad premise*, not against institutional-style scaling into a position you still believe in (the distinction is whether your premise is still valid, not whether the fill price is worse).

**Signature range-extreme scale-in template** (appears repeatedly across chapters): buy the low of a range, add more on each incremental new low (spaced roughly two or more points apart on the Emini), place the aggregate stop below the last add, and target the price of the *first* entry — you exit breakeven on entry one and profitable on the later, lower entries. Mirror at range tops for shorts.

Discipline rule for any countertrend scale-in: exit or reverse on the **second** adverse signal against your position (e.g., a Low 2 forming while you're scaled long against a bear trend) rather than adding a third time.

### Trade management context checks

Two practical self-tests recur throughout: (1) "Would I put this trade on right now, at the market, with this size and this stop, if I were currently flat?" If no, exit the position you're holding — holding is financially identical to re-entering at the current price. (2) Track whether your trader's equation is improving or degrading tick by tick as the trade develops; if it's degrading but still positive, tighten the stop or take a smaller profit; if it turns marginal, exit with whatever you can get; if it turns negative, exit immediately, in full, accepting the loss.


## Common Mistakes (Range/Choppy-Market Specific)

- **Trading every High 1/2/3/4 and Low 1/2/3/4 mechanically inside a tight range or barbwire.** This is called out as one of the fastest ways to hand back a week's profits in an hour, because bar-count setups multiply in a range without any of them carrying real edge.
- **Buying the close of a strong bull trend bar at the top of a range, or shorting the close of a strong bear trend bar at the bottom.** This is exactly where the vacuum effect traps weak traders — the strong move into the extreme is usually caused by the *opposing* side stepping aside, not by fresh with-trend conviction, and it typically reverses immediately.
- **Treating a High 2/Low 2 the same way inside a range as inside a trend.** Buying a High 2 near the *top* of a range (correct only in a trend) or shorting a Low 2 near the *bottom* of a range (correct only in a bear trend) is a recurring, explicitly named beginner error.
- **Martingale sizing after consecutive losses.** Doubling or tripling position size hoping for "an overdue winner" is repeatedly flagged as gambling, not trading — because 4, 6, even 10 consecutive losing stop-entries in a tight range or barbwire are common, not rare, and the position size required after that many losses quickly exceeds any trader's real risk tolerance (a worked example: tripling after each of 10 straight losses would require 37,179 contracts on the 11th trade).
- **Believing "a winner is overdue" after several losses.** Brooks: *"a winner is never overdue, and the market usually continues to do what it just did."* Persistence, not mean-reversion, is the base rate — roughly 70%+ in both directions (trend continuation and range continuation).
- **Fading Fibonacci retracements or band touches with blind limit orders** instead of waiting for the level to actually produce a bullish/bearish *close* and then entering on the resulting stop. The cost is a few ticks of worse entry price; the benefit is avoiding far more false signals.
- **Confusing a "reversal pattern" label (double top, double bottom, head-and-shoulders) with an actual reversal.** Roughly 80% of these patterns are continuation flags that resolve with the larger trend, not against it; trading the textbook neckline break without checking trend context is a losing default.
- **Countertrend scalping inside a strong trend "just to stay busy" while waiting for a with-trend setup.** Explicitly warned against — traders who take these scalps routinely end up unable to reverse mentally in time for the much larger with-trend move that follows, and they lose money on the countertrend scalp besides.
- **Cherry-picking among low-probability (swing-style) setups.** A system built on 40%-probability trades is only profitable in aggregate if *every* qualifying signal is taken; skipping the "ugly" ones (which look the least appealing) and taking only the "easy-looking" ones inverts the edge, because traders systematically pick the wrong cherries.
- **Computing your trader's equation using a profit target you don't actually intend to hold for.** If you tell yourself "I'll hold for 4 points" to justify the math, but you actually bail at 1–2 points out of fear every time, the plan's positive equation and your practice's real (negative) equation are two different things — be honest about your actual intended exit.
- **Trading in the middle third of the trading day while price also sits in the middle third of the day's range.** Called a "double uncertainty" zone; best resolved by waiting for a test of the day's high or low rather than forcing a marginal, lower-conviction entry.
- **Needing to "study the chart from different angles" to convince yourself a signal is good.** Brooks treats that very need for extra study as evidence the signal isn't strong enough — the correct action is to skip it, not force a decision.


## Key Rules of Thumb

- **Trend reversal attempts fail ~80% of the time; trading-range breakout attempts also fail ~80% of the time.** These two mirror-image statistics are the mathematical backbone of the entire book: ride pullbacks in a trend, fade extremes in a range.
- **A tight trading range caps the achievable edge at roughly 51–55% even at the extremes** (absent a preceding climax, which resets it to a genuine 50/50). This is *why* stop entries lose money in tight ranges even when your directional read is correct — there simply isn't enough room to profit.
- **"A tight trading range trumps everything."** No matter how good any other signal looks once you've recognized a tight range, don't trade it on stops; wait for the breakout to fail (or fail again) before acting.
- **Probability tiers for sizing reward vs. risk** (Brooks' own explicit scale): ~70%+ probability needs reward ≥ half of risk to break even; ~60%+ needs reward ≥ risk; ~50% needs reward ≥ 1.5× risk; ~40% or less needs reward ≥ 2× risk. If you're uncertain about a setup's probability, default to 50%; if it "looks good," default to 60/40.
- **Buy low, sell high, and scalp** is the entire range-trading strategy in five words; swing part of a position only on an unusually strong signal at a genuine extreme, ideally aligned with the larger trend's residual bias.
- **Directional bias persists even mid-range** if the range follows a strong prior trend with no clear top/bottom yet — meaning the fade at the range bottom (in an up-trending market) can carry a higher edge (~70–80%) than the fade at the range top (~60%), and vice versa in a downtrend. "You should be more inclined to buy near the bottom of a trading range in a large bull market than you are to short at the top."
- **Ranges tend to break out away from the moving average.** A range sitting below a flat/falling MA favors a downside resolution; one sitting above favors upside; a range far from the MA may instead be setting up a test back *to* the MA rather than an immediate breakout.
- **The final leg of a range within a bull trend is usually a bear channel (a bull flag in disguise); the final leg of a range within a bear trend is usually a bull channel (a bear flag in disguise).** Watch for this last-gasp channel and expect it to fail into a resumption, not a reversal, most of the time.
- **Second entries beat first entries.** A failed setup that fails again (a "failed failure") is more reliable than the original signal, because it traps and then squeezes the traders who took the first attempt.
- **Never add to a losing position** — except when scaling into a pullback within a confirmed trend, or fading a defined range extreme, both according to a plan made *before* you enter, sized so the fully-scaled worst case still matches your normal single-trade risk.
- **Exit or reverse on the second adverse signal**, whether that's a trend resuming against a countertrend scalp, or a range extreme giving a second failure against a scaled-in fade.
- **The market rewards you with roughly as many ticks of profit as it forced you to risk.** Track the largest stop a trade actually needed to survive and use that distance as a working estimate for the day's realistic profit potential.
- **Two reasons, minimum, before entering** — except for with-trend pullbacks in a confirmed strong trend, a strong trend-line-overshoot reversal, or any second entry.
- **Ignore the news, ignore indicators beyond a 20-period EMA, and never assume the market is "due" for anything.** The chart already contains everything relevant; unsustainable-looking behavior (a range, a trend) "can last longer than you can stay solvent betting against it."
